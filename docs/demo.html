Why it confuses those particular words

Look at what it swaps. These becomes this. Thoughts becomes thought. These are not random errors — they are words that look almost identical on the mouth.

p · b · m  →  lips together, then apart
“pat”, “bat” and “mat” are the same video.

The difference between them is voicing and airflow, both of which happen inside the throat where no camera can see. English has around 44 distinct sounds but only about a dozen distinguishable mouth shapes, so entire groups of words collapse into one another. Human lip-readers hit roughly 30–45% word accuracy on unconstrained speech for the same reason. Context has to do the rest of the work — for the machine as much as for a person.

It rarely half-works

The failure pattern is not a gentle slope. Clips cluster at the ends: read perfectly, or lost entirely. 461 clips came in under 10% word errors, while 193 were worse than 100% — the model inventing more words than were spoken.

When it cannot see the mouth

On real footage the face turns away, blurs, or leaves frame. VoxLens marks those stretches rather than quietly inventing words to fill them. This is one 8.3-second clip; the marked bands are the moments it was reading nothing.

0s2s4s6s8s

6 unreadable stretches, 61 of 208 frames. The longest ran 0.6 seconds — long enough to lose several words. The tool reports where they fall, and stops short of claiming which words they cost, because that would mean pretending to a precision it does not have.

What this is measured on. The LRS3 benchmark test split, 50 minutes of video, 9,890 words, greedy decoding. Benchmark video is cleanly shot and cleanly cropped. On in-the-wild footage the same model scores closer to 48% word errors — roughly every other word — so treat the number above as the optimistic end.

These figures are a regression baseline for this pipeline measured against itself, not a claim of parity with published research.
